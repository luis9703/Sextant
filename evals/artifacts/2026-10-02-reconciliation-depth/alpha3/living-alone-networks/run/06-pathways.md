# Pathways (conditional futures, horizon about 2041)

These paths can overlap. They describe routes by which independent residence and dependable connection might be organized; they are not mutually exclusive forecasts or a ranking.

## A. Separate homes, shared “people”

**Ordinary day (illustrative):** Lin lives alone and keeps her own routines. A small circle nearby does not share a home, but they have a regular Sunday meal, hold spare keys by consent, and ask who can accompany someone to a clinic or stay with a pet. On an ordinary week, help is small and reciprocal: a ride, groceries, a phone call after a medical appointment. A neighborhood worker or trusted volunteer can help route needs to formal services when a friend cannot.

**Transition:** repeated, low-stakes contact builds trust before crisis; people agree on what they will and will not do; someone local coordinates handoffs. This pattern becomes more durable if housing and work keep some members nearby and community services supplement their capacity. Limited legal instruments such as advance designation of a guardian can clarify one role, while remaining narrower than spousal/family status.

**Human response and feedback:** choice and privacy remain valuable, while being reliably expected by others can create belonging and a sense of mattering. A helper who receives help in turn may stay involved. The new scarcity is dependable attention: as members move, work, age or become care recipients themselves, the network needs new members or paid help. If needs become intensive, informal reciprocity may no longer balance.

**Stall/reverse:** one person does most of the emotional labor; members avoid explicit commitments; disputes over money or medical decisions surface; or the trusted coordinator exits. Members then use episodic paid care, return to relatives, or let the circle loosen. The path need not mature into a legally recognized “chosen family.”

**Closest existing account:** chosen/fictive kin and community mutual-aid networks. The 2026 Shanghai co-living ethnography finds peer “family” meaning under collective goods and egalitarian decision-making but also disagreement over family matters; this path extends the question to separately housed ties, which that two-house study does not establish (https://doi.org/10.1177/00977004261455410). The 2025 Shanghai older-partner study describes human coordinators linking recipients, neighbors, families and institutions; it concerns older-adult care rather than voluntary peer family (https://doi.org/10.1145/3711058).

## B. Private doors, shared rooms

**Ordinary day (illustrative):** a resident in an intergenerational housing cluster eats alone when she wants, then joins a shared dinner or borrows a tool from the common room. Residents elect stewards, publish the boundaries of shared duties, and pay for cleaning or care tasks that cannot fairly remain volunteer work. Friends may visit without becoming tenants or kin.

**Transition:** housing providers, cooperatives, residents or local governments make common spaces and group governance practical; people form ties while a building or cluster is being designed or managed. A crisis in loneliness, housing cost or caregiving could motivate experimentation, but affordability alone does not guarantee community. Tenure needs to let residents remain long enough to develop trust. Clear rules allow neighbors to help without an assumption of round-the-clock care.

**Human response and feedback:** common meals and shared projects can create repeated contact and belonging; the group may become a chosen family for some. For others the arrangement is a convenient building, not a deep identity. As residents age or family circumstances change, shared routines may shift from recreation toward care; that can strengthen solidarity or make participation feel compulsory. Governance time and conflict resolution become a new scarce resource.

**Stall/reverse:** property costs or rent exclude people with less money; an owner can withdraw common space; a few highly active residents absorb management; unequal care work reproduces gendered expectations; or residents want more privacy than the group expects. The cluster may then become ordinary apartments with amenities, or dissolve as residents move.

**Closest existing account:** cohousing: private dwellings plus common space, self-management and shared activities. A 2020 scoping review found suggestive benefits for support and isolation but only 24 included studies and an uneven evidence base, often with weak comparisons (https://doi.org/10.1186/s40985-020-00138-1). Shanghai youth co-living is a nearer Chinese example of peer belonging but is shared housing, not a proven replicable design for the future (https://doi.org/10.1177/00977004261455410).

## C. Support follows the person; services carry the heavy load

**Ordinary day (illustrative):** after moving cities, a person keeps close friends at a distance and builds a modest local routine. A consent-based contact list distinguishes “call if I miss a check-in” from “may accompany me,” and local paid/community services handle transport or home help. A friend offers conversation and occasional practical help; the system does not assume a friend can provide nursing or become a legal proxy.

**Transition:** households and service providers make contact, consent and referral processes usable across moves; public or paid services remain reachable; coordinators know how to convert a request into an actual appointment or visit. Existing care systems already combine family, neighborhood committees, hospitals, volunteers and technologies. The future change would be better handoffs and recognition of non-kin contacts, not merely more monitoring devices.

**Human response and feedback:** people can remain mobile and independent while maintaining stable relationships. A paid floor lets friendship be freely offered instead of silently conscripted. Yet turning every need into a transaction can leave companionship, trust and ordinary belonging unaddressed. Privacy and control become contested if safety data are collected without clear benefit or if contact permissions do not travel.

**Stall/reverse:** fragmented databases, inaccessible services, high costs, low digital access or institutional rules that recognize only relatives make the network fail at exactly the high-stakes moment. Then wealthier people buy reliable care while others rely on distant relatives, accept unwanted co-residence, or experience unsupported isolation. A documented Shanghai study found care apps and sensor systems could fail to share data or complete a hospital handoff; human “older partners” made the actual connections (https://doi.org/10.1145/3711058). Urban volunteer research also found valued emotional support but difficulties sustaining volunteers, recommending formal services remain a foundation (https://doi.org/10.1007/s44282-024-00120-1).

**Closest existing account:** aging-in-place “mixed economy of care,” plus portability and advance planning. This pathway generalizes from current older-adult settings to people at different ages and allows non-marital ties to remain emotionally meaningful without taking on all care. It is a conditional extension, not an observed 2041 service model.

## Conditions that affect all paths

- “Family” may shift in everyday language and routine before law or benefits change. Current written adult-guardian designation is one limited tool, not general equivalence to marriage or next-of-kin status (Civil Code art. 33, https://fgw.sh.gov.cn/cmsres/98/98301d29f26f462dba5ac280b2180c79/0398f64d92c81f5982924d8e107e67c9.pdf).
- A smaller average Chinese household (2.62 in 2020, down from 3.10 in 2010) is a factual foothold, but does not by itself show a rise in single-person households, chosen-family ties or mutual aid (NBS, https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1901080.html).
- People who want stability may also want an exit; needing help may change over time. A durable arrangement therefore needs consent, boundaries, conflict handling and a path to paid/professional support, not only affection.
- Evidence does not settle whether urban housing affordability, community governance, informal networks or legal treatment will be the binding condition in a particular city. The branch depends on these local conditions.
