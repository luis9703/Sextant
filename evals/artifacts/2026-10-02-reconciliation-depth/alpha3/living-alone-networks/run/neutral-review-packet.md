# Neutral review packet

## Question and scope

Question: “/sextant 我身边越来越多人选择一个人住，也有人不结婚但仍然想拥有稳定的陪伴和互助。假如这种生活方式继续发展，未来十五年的家庭、社区和日常生活会是什么样？帮我想象一下。”

Information cutoff: 2026-10-02. Horizon: about 2041, conditional rather than a date-specific forecast. Working setting: Chinese-speaking urban life, with local and non-urban differences unresolved; user supplied no location. The opening observation is anecdotal and not a measured population claim. “Living alone” refers to residence/household arrangement; it does not mean loneliness. Non-marriage, companionship, household composition, care and legal status are separate. Review should test factual support, missing transitions, competing causal explanations, omitted experiences, and what observations could distinguish paths. No probability estimates are asserted.

## Candidate paths and conditions

### A. Separate homes, shared “people”
A small nearby network of friends, neighbors or other chosen ties builds recurring contact, mutual practical help and explicit boundaries while retaining separate homes.

Conditions: repeated low-stakes interaction; consent and reciprocity; local coordination; links to formal/paid services for intensive needs; enough residential stability or network renewal. Risks/counterevidence: members move or age; uneven emotional labor; reluctance to formalize duties; severe care needs exceed reciprocity. A smaller average household does not establish this mechanism.

Support: China NBS reports average household size of 2.62 in 2020 versus 3.10 in 2010, and attributes shrinkage partly to mobility and separate residence of newly married couples; this does not measure solo living motives or support ties: https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1901080.html. A Shanghai ethnography of two co-living houses reports youth describing roommates as family and pooling common goods, alongside differing/conflicting family expectations: https://doi.org/10.1177/00977004261455410. A specific Shanghai older-adult care ethnography describes retired “older partners” coordinating 5–10 recipients each with neighbors and institutions; its subjects and needs differ from younger chosen kin: https://doi.org/10.1145/3711058.

### B. Private doors, shared rooms
Purpose-built or self-organized housing clusters combine private homes and common rooms/activities governed by residents; repeated shared use may create belonging and practical mutual support.

Conditions: affordable, stable tenure; common spaces residents can govern; explicit fair duties and conflict handling; optional participation and credible exit; paid services as care needs grow. Risks/counterevidence: shared housing can impose unwanted intimacy; costs and governance exclude some; common space can be withdrawn; informal care labor can fall unevenly; cohousing evidence is limited and populations differ.

Support: scoping review of cohousing found indications of social support and reduced isolation in some settings but included only 24 studies from 2,983 identified and noted uneven evidence: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40985-020-00138-1. Shanghai two-house co-living ethnography offers direct but narrow evidence of shared identity and conflict: https://doi.org/10.1177/00977004261455410.

### C. Support follows the person; services carry the heavy load
A mobile person keeps stable, consent-based ties across distance and uses local community or paid services for transport, home help and intensive care. Tools coordinate permissions and handoffs but do not substitute for relationships or care workers.

Conditions: services are affordable and available; referrals work across providers and moves; clear consent/privacy; people can designate contacts for limited roles; human coordinators resolve exceptions. Risks/counterevidence: fragmented systems can collect data without arranging help; digital exclusion/surveillance; market access stratifies support; friendship becomes transactional; volunteer systems can be difficult to sustain.

Support: Shanghai study documents disconnected care systems and technology that could fail to complete a hospital handoff, while human coordinators connected actors: https://doi.org/10.1145/3711058. Urban Chinese volunteer study (39 interviews, Beijing/Guangzhou) found emotional value and sustainability challenges; authors recommend formal services as a foundation: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44282-024-00120-1. Current Chinese Civil Code art. 33 allows an adult with full capacity to designate an advance guardian in writing, a limited tool that does not create general spousal/kin status: https://fgw.sh.gov.cn/cmsres/98/98301d29f26f462dba5ac280b2180c79/0398f64d92c81f5982924d8e107e67c9.pdf.

### D. Low-obligation autonomy as a valid outcome
Some people prefer solo residence, easy exit and episodic/professional help rather than dense mutual obligations. This can coexist with A–C and should not automatically be classified as failed community.

Conditions: affordable and dignified formal support, plus optional social connection. Risks/counterevidence: if affordable services are absent, nominal preference for privacy can mask constrained isolation; strong community expectations may also erase autonomy. Later-life cohabitation research finds some couples use written agreements while some reject formality and want easy exit: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12062-026-09558-4. Volunteer sustainability and need for formal service capacity are documented here: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44282-024-00120-1.

## Evidence limits

NBS household-size data are a broad foothold only. The 2026 Shanghai co-living and later-life cohabitation papers are narrow qualitative settings and do not estimate prevalence. The cohousing review is a scoping review with limited underlying comparative evidence. Older-adult care cases provide mechanisms and failure conditions, not proof of diffusion to all ages. Two PMC full-text pages were blocked by recaptcha in the producing run; the abstracts/index results only were seen and are not relied on here. OECD linked PDF returned 404 and no OECD quantitative comparison is used.
