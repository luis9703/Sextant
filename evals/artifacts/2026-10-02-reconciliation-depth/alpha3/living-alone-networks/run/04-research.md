# Research: challenges and new material

Access date for all links: 2026-10-02.

1. **Population baseline, not the user's exact trend.** NBS Seventh Census release (2021), https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202302/t20230203_1901080.html reports household size fell from 3.10 (2010) to 2.62 (2020), citing mobility and housing conditions among explanations. The release is a census summary, but household size is an aggregate and does not identify living-alone counts, relationship forms, or motives. Keep the user's personal observation separate.
2. **Chosen family in actual Chinese co-living.** Haoyan Zhuang, “The Family We Created: The Sense of Family Among Chinese Co-Living Youth,” *Modern China*, online-first 2026, https://doi.org/10.1177/00977004261455410. Abstract reports ethnographic fieldwork in two Shanghai co-living houses; residents often named roommates as family and collectively funded shared goods, while family-related expectations also diverged or conflicted. This is direct but two-house ethnography of co-residents, not a representative study of separate-home friendship networks or durable outcomes.
3. **Alternative current arrangement and negotiation costs.** 2026 article, “Shared Roofs and Equal Roles? Navigating Gender in Later-life Intimacy through Cohabitation in Urban China,” *Journal of Population Ageing*, https://doi.org/10.1007/s12062-026-09558-4. The article describes some older cohabitors negotiating written agreements over finances, daily labor and emergency care; it also documents some rejecting formality and wanting easy exit. It reports absent civil partnership recognition and legal protections comparable to marriage as a source of continued negotiation. This concerns later-life cohabiting couples, not all non-married people and not a legal opinion for individual cases.
4. **Human coordination as infrastructure.** Sun et al., “Rethinking Technological Solutions for Community-Based Older Adult Care: Insights from 'Older Partners' in China,” PACM HCI/CSCW 2025, https://doi.org/10.1145/3711058 (preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23609). Ethnographic study of 24 stakeholders in a Shanghai care setting describes retired “older partners” each connected to roughly 5–10 older recipients, linking neighbors, family, hospitals and community committees. Technology systems were fragmented, could collect redundant data, and sometimes could not schedule a needed hospital service; non-quantifiable social needs were hard to capture. Authors argue tools should support, not substitute for, human care infrastructure. This is a specific aging-in-place case, not proof a similar design will work for younger adults.
5. **Volunteer systems can be emotionally valuable and fragile.** Cheung, “Volunteer mobilisation and old-age support in urban China,” *Discover Global Society* (2024), https://doi.org/10.1007/s44282-024-00120-1. In 39 qualitative interviews in Beijing/Guangzhou, older interviewees valued volunteers’ emotional support, while the paper describes difficulty sustaining mutual help and recommends coordinating local volunteers and keeping formal services as a foundation. Qualitative settings bound transfer; it cautions against assuming an unpaid network can bear the whole care burden.
6. **Designed shared living has promise but limited evidence.** Berto & Barbiero et al., “The effects of cohousing model on people’s health and wellbeing: a scoping review,” *Public Health Reviews* 41 (2020), https://doi.org/10.1186/s40985-020-00138-1. Of 2,983 records identified, 24 studies were included; authors found indications of social support and lower isolation in some cohousing studies, while evidence across populations and health outcomes was modest/unclear and study designs often lacked comparison groups. Closest existing account to the spatial pathway; it suggests conditions to explore, not general proof.
7. **Formal systems remain a branch condition.** Civil Code art. 33 permits a fully capable adult to determine in writing a guardian with an eligible individual/organization in advance (official Shanghai government-hosted text, https://fgw.sh.gov.cn/cmsres/98/98301d29f26f462dba5ac280b2180c79/0398f64d92c81f5982924d8e107e67c9.pdf). This narrow adult-guardianship mechanism does not make a friend a spouse, grant a general family status, or settle inheritance, benefits, housing or every emergency decision. It means the legal gap is not absolute, but depends on specific instruments and implementation.
8. **Research access limits.** Opened the NBS release, the co-living and later-life cohabitation article pages, the Springer volunteer study, the ACM preprint, and the cohousing scoping review. PubMed Central article pages for the oldest-old qualitative study and mutual-support cross-sectional study were blocked by recaptcha; their abstracts/search-index records were visible and treated as lower-resolution evidence in the foothold only. The OECD linked PDF returned 404; no OECD numerical comparison is used.

### Counterevidence / limits on an easy story

- A smaller average household is consistent with multiple processes: mobility, couples living separately from parents, age structure, and possibly solo living. It does not show that friendship-based mutual aid is expanding.
- Intentional co-residence can create belonging, but roommates can disagree over responsibility and family meaning. Proximity does not automatically create trust or durable care.
- Existing urban volunteer programs depend on local organizers, retirees' time, formal service capacity and handoffs. The network can become unpaid labor or disappear as members age/move.
- Digital systems may make alerts and requests easier while failing at practical service coordination or invading privacy. The relevant enabling condition is a responsive human/service network, not technology availability alone.
- Some people prefer autonomy, looseness and the ability to exit; turning every friendship into a duty-bearing collective would undermine the freedom the arrangement was meant to protect.
