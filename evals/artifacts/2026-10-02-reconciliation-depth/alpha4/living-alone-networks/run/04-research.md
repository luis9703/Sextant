# 04 Research — challenge and new material

Research was bounded to official census/law/policy records and directly relevant studies or observed community designs. Search date/access date: 2026-10-02. Search was not exhaustive; no city-level interviews or housing/operator data were obtained.

## Sources and what they establish

- **R1 — Seventh Census, 2020, household sizes (primary official data).** National Bureau of Statistics table 5-1 reports 58,396,327 one-person households out of 401,934,196 household units; table 5-1a reports 23,099,057 in urban areas. It is a 2020 household count, not intent, marital status, or trend proof. Page was opened and read. https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/pcsj/rkpc/6rp/html/A0501a.htm
- **R2 — Civil Code, article 33 (primary legal text).** Adult advance designation of a guardian is available in a written agreement with a willing person or organization. It is a specific legal role activated by loss of capacity; it does not itself establish a “chosen family” status or settle co-ownership, inheritance, routine medical consent, or service provision. Supreme People's Court published text was returned by search; opening the page produced an internal access error, so the quoted scope is treated as verified against indexed official text, with page-open limitation. https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/233181.html
- **R3 — State Council General Office, 2024 silver economy opinion (primary policy).** Text encourages meal support, home visits, community bathing points, professional companionship, property and home-service provider roles, one-quarter-hour community services, and links among home/community/institutional care. It states intended policy direction, not implementation or outcomes. Opened and read. https://app.www.gov.cn/govdata/gov/202401/15/511180/article.html
- **R4 — 2024 older-adult service statistical bulletin (official repost of government bulletin).** Search-indexed text reports 366,000 community elder-care institutions/facilities at end-2024. This is facility count, not capacity, access, quality, or availability to a particular adult; not opened in full. https://www.dhlc.gov.cn/czz/Web/_F0_0_69TZZV06CAE57ACA879642D18F.htm
- **R5 — “Limited Social Support, but Comparable Health Literacy and Service Utilization, Among Elderly People Living Alone,” China CDC Weekly, six provinces/regions, May–Sep 2020 (primary cross-sectional study).** Search-indexed abstract says 2,959 adults aged 60+, 260 (8.8%) lived alone; those living alone had lower GP registration and less social support, while health literacy and service utilization were comparable. It motivates a counterpath in which separate residence exposes service gaps. The six-area sample and older population cannot generalize to urban younger solo residents or prove causality. Page open hit a browser CAPTCHA; only indexed abstract/text was available. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11532512/
- **R6 — Zuo et al., 2023, systematic review/meta-analysis of loneliness factors among older adults in China.** PubMed abstract reports 15 studies and 47,066 participants; multiple factors were implicated, including marital and living arrangement, family support/relationships, health, finances, housing satisfaction, and social activity. This argues against “marriage status alone solves loneliness.” It is an older-adult synthesis through March 2022, not a study of chosen-family interventions or younger adults. Abstract page read. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36270596/
- **R7 — Ji & Hu, “Infrastructuring Pop-Up Cities with ‘Social Layer’,” arXiv preprint posted 2025-11-19 (research-through-design, authors’ platform).** The paper describes deployments in Jilin and four other locations lasting roughly 6–47 days, using shared calendars, event creation, discovery, and light identity/trust scaffolding. It reports self-organized events and argues infrastructure can lower coordination friction in temporary intentional communities. This offers a concrete mechanism for how weak ties and routines may form, but samples are self-selected, short-lived, and socially oriented; platform authors are evaluating their own system. It does not show durable care obligations or ordinary residents adopting the model. Full arXiv page and its reported deployment details read. https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15680
- **R8 — “Living arrangements of the elderly in China: evidence from the CHARLS national baseline,” 2016 journal article.** The indexed abstract/search material notes that non-co-residence does not preclude children from supporting elders, with some children living in the same neighborhood. This is an important existing baseline: household size and family support are not interchangeable. The PMC page returned CAPTCHA, so the page was not read in full; use only as a lead and do not depend on its quantitative results. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4865266/

## Challenges and unfamiliar material

The case for different household shapes is not the case for abundant reliable care. Current legal and policy footholds allocate narrow roles and encourage services, while studies of older adults identify support gaps and multiple interacting determinants of loneliness. A robust future therefore needs repeated contact, clear consent and responsibility, accessible services, and a way to replace helpers when people move, age, or withdraw.

The closest baseline is already mixed: family members can support across separate homes; community services are expanding in policy; senior mutual aid and shared housing are existing ideas. A materially different claim cannot be “people form communities” by relabeling these. The distinctive mechanism worth exploring is **opt-in, small-scale continuity infrastructure** that makes ordinary help visible while preserving private doors and allowing exit. The pop-up-city study supports the coordination mechanism only at temporary event scale. Long-duration care and broad adoption remain unverified.

Negative/alternative evidence: cohort data on older solo residents show lower GP registration/social support; friendship support can be uneven; fixed proximity does not solve care needs or economic access; shared governance can create exclusion and labor inequity. No evidence located establishes universal desire for co-living or a stable population shift away from marriage.
