# Pathways
These are overlapping pathways, not mutually exclusive forecasts.

## A. Local-tool abundance, with a maintenance layer
A worker starts by automating a repeated reconciliation, scheduling, or reporting task. Better agents plus connectors lower the cost of describing a workflow and producing a prototype. If reusable components, permissions, logs, testing and version updates become accessible, a tool can move from “my script” to a team utility. Coworkers contribute edge cases and requirements; the maker becomes a process steward, and specialists spend less time on small queues and more on architecture, safety and difficult exceptions. Firms may organize around small teams with locally adapted tools, while central IT supplies approved identity, data access, monitoring and recovery rails. The new bottleneck is not code but knowing when a tool is safe, who maintains it, and who answers when upstream systems change. Feedback can make teams more capable and less dependent on central ticket queues; it can also multiply brittle automations and local silos. The path stalls if integration permissions, API changes, hidden maintenance, or accountability outweigh creation savings. Closest account: citizen development/low-code; added condition is AI that translates domain intent into working changes across software lifecycle, while the governance layer absorbs the risk. Evidence so far establishes adjacent adoption/use, not this endpoint.

## B. Domain expertise becomes executable and changes job boundaries
In a more modest but consequential branch, AI does not replace occupational knowledge: it lets people encode fragments of it. A service lead prototypes a triage tool; a planner builds exception alerts; a frontline worker adjusts forms to fit actual practice. If workers can inspect outputs and retain discretion, handoffs shrink and tacit practices become teachable. Novices may learn through guided work, consonant with the bounded customer-support field evidence, while experienced workers shift toward exceptions, quality and mentoring. Status may accrue to people who can articulate a process and judge edge cases, not merely to formal coders. This could broaden agency—or cause management to extract local know-how into standardized workflows and reduce discretion. The branch requires usable evaluation, worker participation, and incentives that reward quality rather than tool counts. It fails if workers cannot understand or override behavior, or if automation captures their expertise without bargaining power. Closest account: task augmentation and knowledge codification; the additional question is whether workers own and govern the executable process.

## C. Assurance becomes the scarce asset and firms centralize the rails
If anyone can generate many plausible apps but few can validate security, privacy, reliability, licensing and support, firms may consolidate around approved platforms and internal “app stores.” Creation decentralizes; execution rights centralize. Employees submit tools for policy checks and reusable components, with role-based access, audit trails, monitoring and named owners. Larger firms could support this layer internally; smaller firms may rely on trusted vendors or managed services. Developers remain important as reviewers, platform builders and responders to incidents, but their work shifts from hand-authoring every feature toward setting boundaries and repairing complex failures. The platform gains leverage through data connectors, identity and distribution. This pathway is conditional on incidents, regulation, customer demands or economics making centralized assurance worth its cost. It can fail if controls are too slow and workers route around them, or if platforms cannot assure what they host. Closest account: enterprise IT governance and platform ecosystems; AI increases the volume and lowers the cost of proposed software, changing the load on assurance.

## D. The backlash and purposeful simplicity branch
If apparently cheap tools create outages, data exposure, invisible maintenance or deskilling without measurable net benefit, organizations and individuals may narrow use. Tools remain personal or disposable; critical workflows return to supported products and accountable specialists. People may choose stable, legible procedures over perpetual customization, and some craft roles become valued precisely because they can explain and maintain systems. This is not technological failure: AI may remain useful for exploration, accessibility or one-off work while production changes require human owners and review. Firms could set protected “no agent action” zones and retain manual fallback. The route requires salient failures or persistent mismatch between output and total lifecycle effort; it is weakened if verification becomes cheap and reliable. Closest account: automation backlash and reliability engineering; additional branch is selective refusal as a source of trust and organizational resilience.

## E. Software becomes situational rather than owned (highly conditional)
If agent systems can safely compose tools on demand, preserve user intent/state, prove permissions, and roll back actions, some interfaces may be generated in context rather than installed as stable apps. A user asks for a temporary view or workflow, uses it, and discards it. Software vendors may sell trusted data/action capabilities and warranties rather than screens; firm boundaries could shift toward whoever owns identity, context and recourse. But this requires much more than code generation: durable semantics, interoperability, permission control, observability, safe failure and support. If any fail, users return to fixed interfaces. This path is a speculative transformation, not evidenced current trend. Closest account: composable software and personal agents; the condition that changes the account is reliable, accountable composition at runtime.

### Discriminating observations to watch
- Track longitudinally what nonprogrammers build, deploy, and still use after 6–12 months; distinguish prototype counts from maintained tools. Sources: workplace audits, opt-in telemetry, surveys with clear occupational sampling. High survival with named owners supports A/B; prototype churn with abandonment supports D.
- Measure total lifecycle effort (creation + review + security + maintenance + incidents) against a comparable no-AI baseline, by task risk and user skill. Time saved in drafting alone does not establish net gains. METR and NBER results differ in setting; replication across nonprogrammer tasks would materially change confidence.
- Observe whether firms create governed internal catalogs, approved connectors, rollback and incident ownership, and whether employees use them or bypass them. Growing centralized rails support C/A; persistent shadow tools support fragmented A or D.
- Measure worker discretion and credit: who can change a tool, contest its output, and receive recognition or compensation for process knowledge? This differentiates empowerment (B) from extraction.
- Count real failures and near misses by severity and exposure, not raw bug reports; pair with deployment volume and risk class. Rising incidents could indicate more use as well as worse safety, so denominators and alternative explanations matter.
- Watch for task-level runtime composition with auditable permissions, rollback and recourse. Generated UI demos alone do not support E.

The research search did not produce robust population data on nonprogrammer tool retention or firm redesign; these signposts remain proposals for measurement, not available indicators. Paths can coexist by risk level and organization size.
